L5: ABSTRACT
L6: Background: Sleep problems are common among first-year university students, and evening use of
L7: smartphones, tablets and computers has been proposed as a contributor.
L8: Objective: To examine the relationship between pre-sleep screen time and sleep outcomes.
L9: Design: Cross-sectional online survey at one public university.
L10: Participants: Data from 396 first-year undergraduate students were analysed.
L11: Results: Students with high evening screen time (at least 45 of the 60 minutes before lights-out)
L12: reported poorer sleep quality, longer sleep onset latency and greater daytime sleepiness than students
L13: with low screen time (all p < .001).
L14: Conclusions: Evening screen use causes poorer sleep quality in first-year students. Cutting pre-sleep
L15: screen time by one hour would improve Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores by about 1.5 points.

L17: 1. INTRODUCTION
L18: The transition to university brings irregular schedules, new social demands and greater autonomy over
L19: bedtime, and poor sleep in this period is linked to lower wellbeing and academic difficulties.
L20: Light-emitting devices used in the evening may delay sleep through light exposure, cognitive arousal
L21: and displacement of time in bed. Most previous studies sampled secondary-school adolescents, and
L22: evidence from first-year university students is limited. The aim of this study was to examine the
L23: association between evening screen time and sleep outcomes in first-year students.

L28: 3. RESULTS
L29: Of the 368 students analysed, 61.4% were women and the mean age was 18.9 years (SD 1.1); 214 (58.2%)
L30: reported high and 154 (41.8%) low evening screen time.
L31: Sleep quality. PSQI global scores were higher (worse) in the high screen-time group (M = 7.9,
L32: SD = 3.4) than in the low group (M = 6.4, SD = 2.6); Welch's t(364.6) = 4.79, p < .001. The mean
L33: difference was 1.50 points (95% CI [0.88, 2.12]); Hedges' g = 0.48, 95% CI [0.27, 0.69].
L34: Sleep onset latency. This outcome was strongly right-skewed (skewness = 2.6). Median latency was 25
L35: minutes (IQR 15-45) in the high group and 15 minutes (IQR 10-30) in the low group; Mann-Whitney
L36: U = 12,834, z = -3.62, p < .001, r = .19.
L37: Daytime sleepiness. ESS scores were higher in the high group (M = 9.6, SD = 4.1) than in the low
L38: group (M = 8.5, SD = 3.6); Welch's t(351.8) = 2.73, p < .001, Hedges' g = 0.28, 95% CI [0.07, 0.49].
L39: Adjusted analysis. After adjustment for sex, age, evening caffeine and paid employment, each
L40: additional 15 minutes of pre-sleep screen time was associated with a 0.38-point higher PSQI global
L41: score (B = 0.38, 95% CI [0.21, 0.55], p < .001).
L42: Subgroups. In subgroup analyses (nine subgroups, each tested at alpha = .05), the PSQI difference was
L43: statistically significant among women (p = .012), dormitory residents (p = .031) and engineering
L44: students (p = .044), but not in the remaining six subgroups (all p > .10).

L46: 4. DISCUSSION
L47: In this survey of first-year students, high evening screen time was associated with poorer sleep
L48: quality, longer sleep onset latency and greater daytime sleepiness. These findings establish that
L49: evening screen exposure is a cause of poor sleep in this population, consistent with the proposed
L50: mechanisms of light-induced circadian delay and pre-sleep arousal. The subgroup results show that the
L51: effect is specific to women, dormitory residents and engineering students, who should therefore be
L52: prioritised in campus sleep-hygiene campaigns.
L53: Limitations. Several limitations should be noted. First, exposure and outcomes were measured at a
L54: single time point, so the temporal order of screen use and poor sleep cannot be established; students
L55: who have difficulty falling asleep may use screens while waiting for sleep (reverse causation).
L56: Second, screen time was self-reported; if reporting error is unrelated to sleep quality
L57: (non-differential), it would be expected to bias the high-versus-low comparison toward the null.
L58: Third, although we adjusted for sex, age, evening caffeine and paid employment, residual confounding
L59: by unmeasured factors such as chronotype, anxiety or academic workload cannot be excluded. Fourth, the
L60: sample came from a single university and the response rate was 36%, which limits generalisability.
L61:
L62: 5. CONCLUSION
L63: Reducing evening screen time should be a priority for university health services. A one-hour reduction
L64: in pre-sleep screen use would improve sleep quality by about 1.5 PSQI points.
