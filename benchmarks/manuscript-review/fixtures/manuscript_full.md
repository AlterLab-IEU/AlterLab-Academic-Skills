L2: Title: Evening Screen Time Causes Poorer Sleep in First-Year University Students: A Cross-Sectional Survey
L3: Authors: [removed for anonymous review]
L4:
L5: ABSTRACT
L6: Background: Sleep problems are common among first-year university students, and evening use of
L7: smartphones, tablets and computers has been proposed as a contributor.
L8: Objective: To examine the relationship between pre-sleep screen time and sleep outcomes.
L9: Design: Cross-sectional online survey at one public university.
L10: Participants: Data from 396 first-year undergraduate students were analysed.
L11: Results: Students with high evening screen time (at least 45 of the 60 minutes before lights-out)
L12: reported poorer sleep quality, longer sleep onset latency and greater daytime sleepiness than students
L13: with low screen time (all p < .001).
L14: Conclusions: Evening screen use causes poorer sleep quality in first-year students. Cutting pre-sleep
L15: screen time by one hour would improve Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores by about 1.5 points.
L16:
L17: 1. INTRODUCTION
L18: The transition to university brings irregular schedules, new social demands and greater autonomy over
L19: bedtime, and poor sleep in this period is linked to lower wellbeing and academic difficulties.
L20: Light-emitting devices used in the evening may delay sleep through light exposure, cognitive arousal
L21: and displacement of time in bed. Most previous studies sampled secondary-school adolescents, and
L22: evidence from first-year university students is limited. The aim of this study was to examine the
L23: association between evening screen time and sleep outcomes in first-year students.
L24:
L25: 2. METHODS
L26: 2.1 Design and participants
L27: We conducted a cross-sectional online survey between 3 and 28 March 2025. All 1,140 first-year
L28: undergraduate students enrolled at the university were invited by e-mail; 412 started the survey
L29: (response rate 36.1%). Forty-four respondents were excluded because their Pittsburgh Sleep Quality
L30: Index (PSQI) responses were incomplete, so that a global score could not be computed, giving a final
L31: analytic sample of 368 students.
L32:
L33: 2.2 Measures
L34: Exposure. Students reported how many of the 60 minutes before lights-out they typically spent using a
L35: smartphone, tablet or computer on weeknights. Following earlier work, we pre-specified a cut-off of 45
L36: minutes to define high (45 min or more) versus low (under 45 min) evening screen time; screen time was
L37: also analysed as a continuous variable.
L38: Outcomes. Six outcomes were measured: (a) sleep quality, PSQI global score (0-21; primary outcome);
L39: (b) sleep onset latency in minutes (PSQI item 2); (c) total sleep time in hours (PSQI item 4);
L40: (d) daytime sleepiness, Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS, 0-24); (e) depressive symptoms, Patient Health
L41: Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9, 0-27); and (f) self-reported grade point average for the first semester.
L42: Covariates were sex, age, caffeine intake after 18:00 (yes/no) and paid employment (10 h/week or more).
L43:
L44: 2.3 Statistical analysis
L45: Sample size: 260 analysable participants (130 per group) were required for 80% power to detect a
L46: standardised difference of d = 0.35 in PSQI global score at two-sided alpha = .05 (G*Power 3.1).
L47: Welch's t-test was pre-specified for all two-group comparisons of approximately symmetric outcomes,
L48: because group sizes were expected to be unequal and Welch's test does not assume equal variances
L49: (Delacre et al., 2017). Outcomes with marked skew (pre-specified criterion: absolute skewness > 2)
L50: were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test and summarised as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR).
L51: Effect sizes were Hedges' g with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for t-tests and r = |z|/sqrt(N) for
L52: Mann-Whitney tests. The adjusted association between continuous screen time and PSQI global score was
L53: estimated by multiple linear regression including all four covariates. Finally, the high-versus-low
L54: PSQI comparison was repeated within subgroups defined by sex (women, men), residence (dormitory,
L55: family home, private flat) and faculty (health, engineering, social sciences, arts), giving nine
L56: subgroup tests. Analyses used R 4.4.1.
L57:
L58: 3. RESULTS
L59: Of the 368 students analysed, 61.4% were women and the mean age was 18.9 years (SD 1.1); 214 (58.2%)
L60: reported high and 154 (41.8%) low evening screen time.
L61: Sleep quality. PSQI global scores were higher (worse) in the high screen-time group (M = 7.9,
L62: SD = 3.4) than in the low group (M = 6.4, SD = 2.6); Welch's t(364.6) = 4.79, p < .001. The mean
L63: difference was 1.50 points (95% CI [0.88, 2.12]); Hedges' g = 0.48, 95% CI [0.27, 0.69].
L64: Sleep onset latency. This outcome was strongly right-skewed (skewness = 2.6). Median latency was 25
L65: minutes (IQR 15-45) in the high group and 15 minutes (IQR 10-30) in the low group; Mann-Whitney
L66: U = 12,834, z = -3.62, p < .001, r = .19.
L67: Daytime sleepiness. ESS scores were higher in the high group (M = 9.6, SD = 4.1) than in the low
L68: group (M = 8.5, SD = 3.6); Welch's t(351.8) = 2.73, p < .001, Hedges' g = 0.28, 95% CI [0.07, 0.49].
L69: Adjusted analysis. After adjustment for sex, age, evening caffeine and paid employment, each
L70: additional 15 minutes of pre-sleep screen time was associated with a 0.38-point higher PSQI global
L71: score (B = 0.38, 95% CI [0.21, 0.55], p < .001).
L72: Subgroups. In subgroup analyses (nine subgroups, each tested at alpha = .05), the PSQI difference was
L73: statistically significant among women (p = .012), dormitory residents (p = .031) and engineering
L74: students (p = .044), but not in the remaining six subgroups (all p > .10).
L75:
L76: 4. DISCUSSION
L77: In this survey of first-year students, high evening screen time was associated with poorer sleep
L78: quality, longer sleep onset latency and greater daytime sleepiness. These findings establish that
L79: evening screen exposure is a cause of poor sleep in this population, consistent with the proposed
L80: mechanisms of light-induced circadian delay and pre-sleep arousal. The subgroup results show that the
L81: effect is specific to women, dormitory residents and engineering students, who should therefore be
L82: prioritised in campus sleep-hygiene campaigns.
L83: Limitations. Several limitations should be noted. First, exposure and outcomes were measured at a
L84: single time point, so the temporal order of screen use and poor sleep cannot be established; students
L85: who have difficulty falling asleep may use screens while waiting for sleep (reverse causation).
L86: Second, screen time was self-reported; if reporting error is unrelated to sleep quality
L87: (non-differential), it would be expected to bias the high-versus-low comparison toward the null.
L88: Third, although we adjusted for sex, age, evening caffeine and paid employment, residual confounding
L89: by unmeasured factors such as chronotype, anxiety or academic workload cannot be excluded. Fourth, the
L90: sample came from a single university and the response rate was 36%, which limits generalisability.
L91:
L92: 5. CONCLUSION
L93: Reducing evening screen time should be a priority for university health services. A one-hour reduction
L94: in pre-sleep screen use would improve sleep quality by about 1.5 PSQI points.
