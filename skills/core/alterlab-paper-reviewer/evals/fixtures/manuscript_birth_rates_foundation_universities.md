# Declining Birth Rates and the Management Strategies of Foundation Universities in Turkey: A Mixed-Methods Study

*Fictional manuscript written for a review exercise. The authors, institutions, interviews and fill-rate data are invented.*

Target journal: *Higher Education Policy* (draft for submission)

## Abstract

Turkey's birth rate has fallen sharply over the last decade. This study examines how the
decline has affected foundation (vakıf) universities and how their managers respond. Using
annual data for 2016–2024, we find a strong correlation between the number of live births and
the first-year fill rate of foundation universities (r = .99, p < .001). A regression model
with births, tuition and the number of programmes explains 99% of the variance in fill rates.
Semi-structured interviews with 14 senior managers identified three strategies: cost
containment, programme specialisation and internationalisation. We conclude that the
declining birth rate has caused the fall in demand for foundation universities and that, to
survive, all foundation universities in Turkey must either merge or specialise.

**Keywords:** demographic decline, foundation universities, higher education management, Turkey

## 1. Introduction

Turkey's total fertility rate has fallen below the replacement level, and the number of live
births dropped from about 1.31 million in 2016 to about 0.94 million in 2024 (TÜİK, 2025).
Over the same period, the share of first-year places filled at foundation universities fell
from 96% to 78% (authors' compilation from YÖK Atlas). Foundation universities depend on
tuition income, so a shrinking pool of applicants threatens their financial sustainability.

Although the demographic decline is widely discussed in the Turkish press, no study has
measured its effect on foundation universities or documented how their managers respond.
This study fills that gap with two research questions:

- RQ1: To what extent has the decline in births reduced demand for foundation universities?
- RQ2: What management strategies do foundation university leaders adopt in response?

## 2. Literature Review

Research on Turkish higher education has focused on the rapid expansion of the system since
2006 and on quality assurance. Foundation universities have been studied mainly in terms of
governance and academic freedom. Studies of student demand in Turkey have examined the
central placement exam and tuition levels, but none has linked demand to demographic change.
Mixed-methods designs are recommended when a phenomenon needs both measurement and
explanation (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018), which motivates the present design.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Quantitative strand

We compiled annual national data for 2016–2024 (n = 9 years): live births in each year
(TÜİK, 2025), the mean first-year fill rate of foundation universities (YÖK Atlas), mean
annual tuition in real terms, and the number of undergraduate programmes offered by
foundation universities. We computed the Pearson correlation between births and fill rate
in the same year, and fitted an OLS regression of fill rate on births, tuition and the number
of programmes.

### 3.2 Qualitative strand

We interviewed 12 senior managers (rectors, vice-rectors and secretaries-general) from 12
foundation universities in Istanbul, recruited through the first author's professional
network. Interviews lasted 40–70 minutes and were transcribed verbatim. The first author
coded all transcripts using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006); themes were finalised
when no new codes appeared.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Births and fill rates

| Year | Live births (thousands) | Fill rate, foundation universities (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 2016 | 1,312 | 96 |
| 2017 | 1,291 | 95 |
| 2018 | 1,248 | 93 |
| 2019 | 1,184 | 91 |
| 2020 | 1,113 | 88 |
| 2021 | 1,080 | 86 |
| 2022 | 1,036 | 84 |
| 2023 | 958 | 80 |
| 2024 | 938 | 78 |

Births and fill rates were strongly correlated (r = .99, p < .001). In the regression, births
(β = .71, p = .01) and tuition (β = −.31, p = .04) were significant predictors; the number of
programmes was not (β = .08, p = .61). The model explained 99% of the variance (R² = .99).
These results show that each decline of 100,000 births reduces the fill rate by roughly five
percentage points.

### 4.2 Management strategies

Three themes emerged.

**Cost containment** (mentioned by 10 of 12 managers): freezing hiring, merging departments and
moving part-time teaching online. "We cannot wait for the students to come back; we have
already cut two faculties," said one vice-rector.

**Programme specialisation** (8 of 12): concentrating on high-demand fields such as health
sciences and engineering and closing programmes with low fill rates.

**Internationalisation** (7 of 12): recruiting students from Central Asia, the Middle East and
Africa to replace domestic applicants. Several managers said international recruitment is
"the only growth market left".

## 5. Discussion

Our findings show that the declining birth rate is already reducing demand for foundation
universities. The strong correlation and the high explanatory power of our model leave little
doubt that demography is the main driver of falling fill rates. Managers have recognised this
and are responding with cost cutting, specialisation and international recruitment.

Policy makers should prepare for a wave of closures. We recommend that YÖK require all
foundation universities to merge with another institution or specialise in no more than three
fields by 2030.

**Limitations.** The interviews were conducted in one city. The quantitative series is short.

## 6. Conclusion

The demographic decline has caused a fall in demand for foundation universities in Turkey.
Unless they merge or specialise, many will not survive the coming decade.
