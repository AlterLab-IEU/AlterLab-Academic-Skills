# Methods (excerpt from a manuscript under review)

*Fictional manuscript written for a design-critique exercise. The study, authors and data are invented.*

**Title:** Gratitude journaling reduces burnout in early-career teachers: a controlled trial

**Participants.** We recruited early-career teachers (fewer than five years in post) through an
email to all staff in the first author's school district. Twenty-six teachers volunteered, and
everyone who volunteered was enrolled.

**Allocation.** Participants were allocated in order of sign-up: odd-numbered registrants joined
the journaling programme and even-numbered registrants joined a waitlist (n = 13 per group).

**Intervention.** The programme group wrote down three things they were grateful for each
evening for four weeks and attended a weekly 30-minute group session led by the first author.
The waitlist group had no contact with the study team during the four weeks.

**Blinding.** Neither the participants nor the first author were blinded to group. The first
author delivered the programme and also handed out and collected the post-test questionnaires.

**Outcomes.** The primary outcome was emotional exhaustion on the Maslach Burnout Inventory
(self-report), measured at baseline and at four weeks. Secondary outcomes were
depersonalisation, personal accomplishment, perceived stress, sleep quality, job satisfaction
and intention to leave teaching, all self-reported.

**Sample size.** No a priori power analysis was done; the sample size was set by the number of
volunteers.

**Attrition and analysis.** Three waitlist participants did not return the post-test and were
excluded, so the analysis included 23 teachers (13 programme, 10 waitlist). We compared
post-test scores between groups with an independent-samples t-test for each outcome at
alpha = .05, with no correction for multiple comparisons. Baseline scores were not used in the
analysis.

**Results (summary).** Emotional exhaustion was lower in the programme group at four weeks
(t(21) = 2.17, p = .04, d = 0.91). None of the six secondary outcomes differed significantly.

**Authors' conclusion.** "These results demonstrate that gratitude journaling causes a
meaningful reduction in teacher burnout. Districts should adopt the programme for all
early-career teachers."
